Chapter 10

EvacPrint from zero — understand your project in simple words

Read this chapter aloud to each other once. If you can explain it this simply, you can answer any judge.

The story 200 students are sitting in Seminar Hall 406. A fire alarm goes off. What happens in the next 90 seconds — and how could the hall be changed so that it happens faster?

10.1How does the system know where people are?

We tell it. The hall is drawn as a chessboard of small squares, each 50 cm × 50 cm (about one person's shoulder width). Some squares are seats, some are walls, some are floor, some are doors. When a simulation starts, the computer places each virtual person on a seat square — shuffled randomly but reproducibly, using a "seed" number (406 by default), like shuffling a deck of cards in exactly the same way every time. If there are more people than seats, the rest stand in aisles.

10.2How does it know where the exits are?

Exits are special squares on the chessboard, drawn by us (Hall 406) or by the user (Venue Studio). Each exit has a name and a width. Doors (A, B, C) lead from the hall to the corridor; final exits (Lobby exit, North fire stair, West stair) lead out of the building. A person is "evacuated" only when they pass a final exit.

10.3How does it decide which direction people move?

Imagine you pour water at the exit and it spreads backwards through the hall, square by square, around walls and seats. Every square remembers how far the water had to travel to reach it. That gives a number on every square: "you are 12.4 m from the North fire stair". This is Dijkstra's algorithm. We do it once for every exit.

Each person looks at the numbers around them and steps towards smaller numbers — like a ball rolling downhill. They choose the exit with the smallest number from where they stand. Every 2 seconds the numbers are recalculated with crowds counted as "uphill", so if a door has a big queue, people further away may choose another door — just like real people do.

10.4Why doesn't everyone move at once?

Because real people don't. When the alarm rings, each virtual person waits a personal reaction time (by default somewhere between 10 and 30 seconds) — finishing a sentence, grabbing a bag. Then they stand up and walk at their own speed: a typical adult about 1.34 m/s; someone with reduced mobility or in a wheelchair 0.7–1.0 m/s.

10.5Why does a crowd gather near a narrow door?

A 1-metre door can let through only about 1.33 people per second — one person every 0.75 seconds. If people arrive faster than that, the extras must wait. The waiting people form a curved queue (an "arch") in front of the door. Inside a crowd people also walk slower: the more people per square metre, the shorter your steps. At 2.15 people per square metre, people already walk at less than half their normal speed. So a narrow door causes a queue, the queue causes high density, and high density slows everyone down further.

AnalogyA toll plaza on a highway. Cars arrive from many lanes, but the booth processes one car at a time. When arrivals exceed the booth's capacity, a jam forms behind it — and opening a second booth helps more than making the cars faster.

10.6How does the system identify a bottleneck?

Every tenth of a second, the computer counts how crowded each square is (people per square metre). If a square stays above 2.15 people/m² — what crowd scientist John Fruin called "Level of Service F", the worst level — it starts a stopwatch for that square. After the evacuation, squares that were critical for at least 3 seconds and touch each other are grouped into a zone, given a name like "Door C approach", and ranked by how bad and how long. That zone is a bottleneck.

10.7What happens when an exit becomes unavailable?

If Door B is blocked, it becomes a wall. The distance numbers are recalculated without it, so the 38 people who would have used Door B (and then the Lobby exit) now walk to Doors A or C instead. Those queues get longer. In our model the evacuation goes from 93.6 s to 110.5 s — about 17 seconds slower — and the Lobby exit is not used at all, because the only way to it was through Door B.

10.8How can changing the layout improve evacuation?

EvacPrint tries changes for you: wider doors, an extra exit, moving the AV console, wider aisles, a shorter stage, removing a seat row. For each one it runs the whole evacuation again, three times with three different random crowds, and measures the saving. With Door B blocked, adding a 1-metre emergency exit on the west wall saves about 29 seconds on average — more than even clearing Door B (≈ 13 s), because it opens a brand-new route right where the crowd is. That is the kind of insight a static plan cannot give you.

10.9Why is this a simulation and not an animation?

An animation is drawn in advance — it always looks the same. EvacPrint calculates every person's position every 0.1 seconds from rules. Change one number (block a door, add 100 people, raise panic) and the outcome changes, and the timings are measured, not drawn. The 3D view and the 2D plan are just two cameras pointing at the same calculation.

10.10Where does the "digital twin" idea fit?

A digital twin is a virtual copy of a real place that you can experiment on. Our Hall 406 model and any venue built in Venue Studio are simple digital twins: you can block exits, change crowds and redesign the room without touching the real building. (A full industrial digital twin would also be fed live sensor data — that is future scope for us.)

Chapter 10b

Technical glossary

Plain-language definitions, with how each term appears in EvacPrint.

Digital twinA virtual copy of a real place or system used to test "what if" safely. In EvacPrint: the Hall 406 model and venues built in Venue Studio.
Agent-based modelling (ABM)Simulating many independent individuals ("agents"), each following simple rules; the group behaviour emerges. Each occupant is an agent.
AgentOne simulated person with speed, size, reaction time, target exit and state (waiting, moving, evacuated).
PathfindingFinding a route from one place to another. We compute routes to every exit from every cell at once.
BFS (Breadth-First Search)Explores a graph in rings of equal steps. Works when every step costs the same. Not used for routing in EvacPrint because our steps have different costs.
Dijkstra's algorithmFinds lowest-cost paths when steps have different costs, using a priority queue. EvacPrint's routing core.
A* searchDijkstra plus a "guess" towards one goal; finds one path quickly. Not used — we need all distances to every exit.
Distance field / floor fieldA map giving every cell's cost to reach an exit. Agents walk down its slope.
Grid / cellThe venue divided into 0.5 m × 0.5 m squares; each square has a type (floor, wall, seat, door, exit…).
HeatmapA map coloured by a value — here crowd density: green low, yellow moderate, orange high, red critical.
Crowd densityPeople per square metre (p/m²). Above 2.15 is Fruin LOS F; around 4 and above is crush-risk territory.
Level of Service (LOS, Fruin)A grading of walkway comfort from A (free) to F (severely congested), by density. Our heatmap bands are based on it.
BottleneckA place where flow is limited (usually a door) and density stays high. Detected automatically, named B1, B2…
Throughput / flowHow many people pass a point per second. A 1 m door: 1.33 p/s in our model.
Specific flowFlow per metre of width — 1.33 persons/s/m (SFPE planning value).
RSETRequired Safe Escape Time — time until the last person is out. We measure it.
ASETAvailable Safe Escape Time — how long conditions stay survivable (depends on fire/smoke). Not computed by EvacPrint.
Pre-movement / reaction timeTime between the alarm and a person starting to move (default 10–30 s).
Faster-is-slower effectRushing crowds clog doors and can get out later than calm ones. Modelled in a simplified way through the panic level.
SeedA number that fixes the random choices, so a run can be repeated exactly. Default 406.
SimulationCalculating how a system evolves over time from rules — here in 0.1 s steps.
Real-time analyticsMetrics updated while the simulation runs: evacuated count, peak density, exit use, live bottlenecks.
FrontendWhat runs in the browser: pages, buttons, 2D/3D views — and in our case also the simulation engine.
BackendCode on a server. Ours is a small Node.js layer used only for the AI features (keeps the API key secret).
APIA defined way for programs to talk to each other. Our browser calls /api/assistant; our server calls the Google Gemini API.
DatabaseOrganised stored data. We have no database server; we use the browser's built-in IndexedDB (venues, photos) and localStorage (accounts, reports).
AI / LLMLarge language model — a model that understands and writes text (and here, reads images). We use Google Gemini; we did not train our own model.
Function calling (tools)The AI can ask the app to perform named actions (e.g. run a what-if). EvacPrint shows a Confirm card first.
3D renderingDrawing a 3D scene on a 2D screen. We use Three.js on top of WebGL (the browser's GPU graphics API).
Canvas 2DThe browser's pixel-drawing surface; draws our floor plan and heatmap.
Web WorkerA background thread in the browser; we run many simulations in parallel without freezing the page.
Token bucketA rate limiter: tokens refill at a fixed rate and each passage uses one. How we enforce door capacity.
Kernel density estimateEstimating density by giving each person a smooth "bump" and adding the bumps up (R = 0.7 m).
HysteresisRequiring a clear improvement before switching, to avoid flip-flopping between exits.
ViteThe tool that serves our app during development and bundles it for production.
SSE (Server-Sent Events)A way for the server to stream text to the browser as it is generated (AI answers).
NBC 2016 Part 4India's National Building Code chapter on fire and life safety — the authority for real compliance. EvacPrint does not certify against it.

11.4Backup plan

If…Do this (calmly — say what you are doing)
The internet stops workingNothing changes except AI. The simulator, 3D, recommendations, Venue Studio (except AI drafting) and reports all run locally. Skip step 11 and say: "The AI assistant needs internet; the simulation itself runs fully offline."
The simulation feels slowSwitch to 8×. Or skip straight to Suggest improvements — it does not need a finished live run. Or show the Overview benchmark (4 scenarios computed in under a second). Or open the pre-generated report.
The 3D model does not loadSwitch the Simulator to 2D plan — all analytics still work. If WebGL is unavailable the app shows a message instead of 3D. As a last resort show the 3D screenshots in this handbook.
An upload failsDo not debug on stage. Use the Quick room builder or "Start from a lecture-room template". (DWG is intentionally not supported — export DXF/PDF.)
The AI assistant does not respondWait 10 s once. If it shows an error ("free-tier limit" or "overloaded"), say: "We use Gemini's free tier, which is rate-limited — the simulation does not depend on it." Close the panel and continue.
A button stops workingReload the page (F5). Your demo sign-in persists in the same tab. Use the pre-opened tabs. If the page is blank: in the terminal press Ctrl+C, run npm run dev again, reload.
Wrong state after Apply & comparePress Original in the Layout panel, then pick the scenario card again.
