Chapter 01

Project overview

What EvacPrint is, the problem it addresses, and what we actually built.

EvacPrint is a browser-based crowd-evacuation simulator. You give it a venue — our default is a model of MSIT Seminar Hall 406 — and a crowd. It runs a virtual emergency in which every person is an independent "agent" who reacts to the alarm, chooses an exit, walks, slows down in crowds and queues at doors. It measures how long the evacuation takes, finds where the crowd jams, and then automatically tests design changes (wider doors, an extra exit, cleared obstacles) to recommend the ones that actually get people out faster.

+16.9 sExtra time when Door B is blocked (110.5 s)
−27.2 sSaved in the blocked scenario by the top recommendation (110.5 → 83.3 s)

These numbers are measured by the engine, not typed in. They are reproducible: same seed, same result. They come from a representative model of Hall 406 whose dimensions still need on-site verification, so treat them as comparisons between options, not as certified absolute times.

1.1What problem does it solve?

Every building has an evacuation plan on the wall: a floor plan with green arrows. That plan shows where to go, but it cannot tell you how long it will take, where people will get stuck, or what happens if one exit is blocked by stored chairs. Those answers depend on how real crowds behave — and crowds behave in non-obvious ways: a narrow door creates a queue, a queue slows everyone behind it, and people tend to pick the exit they know rather than the nearest one.

EvacPrint makes those hidden risks visible and measurable before an emergency, using a simulation instead of guesswork.

1.2Why it matters

  • Crowd disasters are usually about flow, not just fire. Crushes and stampedes happen when too many people try to pass a narrow point at once. The danger is the density of people per square metre — something you cannot see on a static floor plan.
  • Seconds matter. Fire-safety engineering compares the time people need to get out (RSET, Required Safe Escape Time) with the time conditions stay survivable. Reducing RSET increases the safety margin.
  • Real drills are expensive and rare, cannot safely test "what if this exit is blocked", and cannot try ten different layouts in an afternoon. A simulation can.

1.4How our approach is different

  1. Agent-based, not an animation. Each of the 200–400 people is computed every 0.1 s. Nothing is pre-recorded.
  2. One engine, many views. The 2D plan, the 3D model, the charts and the report all read the same simulation state.
  3. It recommends, not just visualises. The recommendation engine re-runs the entire evacuation for every design change on 3 random seeds, and only marks a change as an improvement if it helps on every seed.
  4. Honest by design. Every number comes from a run; every result screen states that it is a model estimate; AI-drafted geometry is marked "unverified".
  5. Runs in a browser. No installation for the user, and the simulator itself works offline.

1.5Who can use it?

  • College and school administrators (seminar halls, auditoriums, labs)
  • Event organisers planning seating and crowd size
  • Fire-safety officers preparing for a drill or an inspection
  • Architects and interior designers comparing layouts
  • Students and researchers learning crowd dynamics (the Learn section)
  • Venue managers deciding where to store equipment

1.6Real-world impact

A venue manager can learn, in minutes and at zero cost, that (for example) leaving equipment in front of Door B costs about 17 extra seconds for 200 people, and that adding one 1-metre exit on the west wall would save more time than any other single change tested. That turns a vague feeling ("this hall feels crowded") into a specific, evidence-backed action item — before anyone is in danger.

Chapter 02

Problem statement

The gap between an evacuation plan on paper and an evacuation in reality.

Problem in one sentence Venue managers make decisions — door widths, seating, where to store equipment, how many people to allow — without any way to measure how those decisions affect the time it takes to evacuate everyone, or where a dangerous crowd will form.

2.1An example everyone in the room will recognise

Imagine Seminar Hall 406 during a guest lecture: 200 students seated in rows, a stage at the front, three 1-metre doors leading to corridors and stairs. The fire alarm rings. Nobody moves for the first 10–30 seconds — people finish a sentence, pick up bags, look around. Then everyone stands up at once and heads for "their" door. Three 1-metre doors can only let through about 1.33 people per second each. Within seconds, a queue bends into an arch in front of each door. The people at the back of the queue are not walking any more; they are waiting.

Now imagine one of those doors is blocked by stored chairs. Everyone who would have used it must turn around and join another queue. The plan on the wall does not change — but the evacuation got 17 seconds slower in our model, and the crowd at the remaining doors got denser.

2.2Why this is hard to see without simulation

  • Congestion is emergent. No single person causes a jam; it appears when many independent decisions interact. You can only see it by simulating many people at once.
  • The obvious fix is not always the best one. In our Hall 406 model the worst bottleneck is at Door C, yet widening Door C saves only ≈ 2.8 s on average, while adding a west emergency exit saves ≈ 17.3 s. Intuition would pick the wrong change.
  • People are different. Seniors, wheelchair users and people unfamiliar with the building move and choose routes differently. Averages hide who gets out last.
  • Drills cannot test dangerous variations such as a blocked exit or an over-capacity event safely or repeatedly.

2.3Limitations of traditional evacuation planning

  1. Static: arrows on a plan do not move, queue or slow down.
  2. Rule-of-thumb capacity checks (e.g. "exit width per person") ignore where people actually are and how they converge on one door.
  3. One-off: a plan is drawn once; rearranging seating or storage changes the risk without anyone noticing.
  4. No feedback loop: there is no tool that says "change this, and you save X seconds".

2.4Why our solution is needed

Professional evacuation-modelling software exists, but it is typically expensive desktop software operated by specialists. Colleges, small auditoriums and event organisers need something that runs in a browser, uses established crowd-dynamics research, explains its results in plain language, and suggests concrete fixes. That is the gap EvacPrint targets.

Chapter 03

Our solution

Simulate · Predict · Optimize · Protect.

EvacPrint turns a venue into a digital blueprint (a grid of 0.5 m × 0.5 m cells), fills it with virtual people, sounds a virtual alarm and measures what happens. Then it tries design changes on its own and tells you which ones help.

3.1Main objective

Give anyone responsible for a venue a fast, free, explainable way to answer three questions:

  1. How long would it take to evacuate this venue with this crowd?
  2. Where would dangerous crowding form, and why?
  3. What change to the layout would help the most?

3.2How it helps venue planners

  • Scenario testing: normal day, blocked exit, over-capacity event, mixed-mobility audience — or any custom combination of crowd size, reaction time, panic level and closed exits.
  • Evidence for decisions: "remove the cabinet from in front of Door B" becomes "removing it saves about 13 seconds on average across 3 test runs".
  • Inclusive planning: the population wizard tracks how wheelchair users, seniors and people with walking aids fare compared with everyone else.
  • Your own venue: Venue Studio builds a digital model from a floor plan, from measurements, by drawing it, or (with AI) as a first draft from photos.

3.3The two halves of the product

Simulator (Hall 406) The polished, fast demo path. Four ready scenarios on a representative model of Seminar Hall 406, with live 2D + 3D, analytics, bottlenecks, risk analysis, recommendations, before/after comparison and reports. This is what we demo first.
Venue Studio (any venue) Build your own venue: capture photos, import a floor plan, draw it, or type measurements into the Quick room builder. Configure the population with a 10-step wizard, generate the 3D model, run 3-seed simulations and test design changes.
